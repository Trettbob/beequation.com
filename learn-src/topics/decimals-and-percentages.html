A decimal is a way of writing a number that includes parts of a whole, using a decimal point. In 2.5, the 2 is whole ones and the 5 is five tenths, so 2.5 is two and a half. A percentage is a number out of 100: 50% (fifty per cent) means 50 out of every 100, which is the same as a half. Fractions, decimals and percentages are three ways of writing the same amounts, and by Year 6 children move between them freely.

What children learn, year by year

This follows the National Curriculum in England. US grades are roughly one year behind the UK year number.

The big ideas

1. Decimals carry place value past the ones

Each place value column is worth a tenth of the column to its left, and that pattern carries on after the ones. The decimal point sits between the ones and the tenths. One whole split into 10 equal parts gives tenths, a tenth split into 10 gives hundredths, and a hundredth split into 10 gives thousandths.

Money is a good way in. £3.45 is 3 whole pounds, 4 tenths of a pound (four 10p coins) and 5 hundredths of a pound (five 1p coins).

2. Longer doesn't mean bigger

Is 0.45 bigger than 0.5? Many children say yes, because 45 is bigger than 5. But 0.5 is 5 tenths, and 0.45 is only 4 tenths and 5 hundredths. Writing 0.5 as 0.50 makes it clear: 50 hundredths is more than 45 hundredths. To compare decimals, line up the decimal points and compare column by column from the left, as with whole numbers.

3. Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1,000 moves the digits

When you multiply by 10, every digit moves one place to the left, so it is worth ten times more: 3.6 × 10 = 36. When you divide by 10, every digit moves one place to the right: 3.6 ÷ 10 = 0.36. Multiplying or dividing by 100 moves the digits two places, and by 1,000 three places. The decimal point itself stays put; it is the digits that move.

4. Rounding decimals works the same way

To round to the nearest whole number, look at the tenths digit: 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less rounds down. So 4.68 rounds to 5, and 3.14 rounds to 3. To round to one decimal place, look at the hundredths digit: 4.68 rounds to 4.7, and 3.14 rounds to 3.1. Rounding is useful for estimating with money and measurements.

Shopping is the best decimals practice there is. Ask your child to round prices to the nearest pound and estimate the total before you reach the till: £1.89 + £2.35 + £0.99 is about £2 + £2 + £1 = £5 (the real total is £5.23). Price labels that show "per 100 g" or a percentage off in a sale are good talking points too.

5. Per cent means "out of 100"

"Per cent" means "for every hundred". A hundred square shows it well: each small square is 1%, a whole row of ten is 10%, and the whole square is 100%. Because a percentage is a number of hundredths, it is quick to write as a fraction or a decimal: 35% = ³⁵⁄₁₀₀ = 0.35, and 7% = ⁷⁄₁₀₀ = 0.07.

6. Finding a percentage of an amount

Children build percentages from ones they can find quickly:

  • 50% is half, so divide by 2. 50% of 60 is 30.
  • 25% is a quarter, so divide by 4. 25% of 60 is 15.
  • 10% is a tenth, so divide by 10. 10% of 60 is 6.
  • 1% is a hundredth, so divide by 100. 1% of 600 is 6.

Then they combine them. 30% of 60 is three lots of 10%: 6 × 3 = 18. For 15% of 360: 10% is 36, 5% is half of that, 18, so 15% is 36 + 18 = 54.

Common mistakes to look out for

  • Longer is bigger: thinking 0.45 > 0.5, or that 0.125 is bigger than 0.2.
  • Reading decimals as whole numbers: saying 0.25 as "nought point twenty-five". Saying "nought point two five" keeps the place value clear.
  • Adding a zero to multiply by 10: writing 3.6 × 10 = 3.60. The digits need to move, giving 36.
  • Mixing up tenths and hundredths: writing ⁷⁄₁₀₀ as 0.7 instead of 0.07.
  • Money muddles: reading £2.5 on a calculator as £2.05. It means £2.50.
  • Thinking 0.3 is a third: ⅓ is 0.333… (the 3s go on forever), which is a little more than 0.3.

Five-minute decimal and percentage games for home

  • Price round-up. Pick three things in a shop or online. Round each price to the nearest pound and estimate the total.
  • Measure up. Measure family members in metres. Who is 1.32 m? Who is 1.4 m? Put everyone in order of height.
  • Sale detective. Spot "20% off" or "half price" signs. What would £30 shoes cost with 20% off? (10% is £3, so 20% is £6, and the shoes cost £24.)
  • Battery check. A phone or tablet showing 40% battery has 40 out of 100 left. What fraction is that? (⅖.)
  • Match three. Write fractions, decimals and percentages on cards (¼, 0.25, 25%) and find the sets of three.
