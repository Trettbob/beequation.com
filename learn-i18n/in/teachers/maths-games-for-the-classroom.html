A good maths game makes children use a method, not just wait for a lucky throw. These fifteen games need little more than the blackboard, scrap paper, counters and a few number cards. Choose the Class range as a starting point, then adjust to what your children have already learnt.

Show one round before starting. Let partners explain in any classroom language, and help them connect their explanation to the mathematical words you are teaching. Keep children in the game after a mistake: a second attempt tells you much more than elimination does.

Give thinking time before taking an answer. Ask pairs to show a drawing, grouping or calculation as well as the result. Speed need not decide who wins.

1. Honeycomb hunt — Classes 1 to 5

Write five numbers in separate cells. Children find three that make an addition statement, or a multiplication statement if that is the chosen rule. For an addition round, use 4, 8, 10, 6 and 5: 4 + 6 = 10. The other cells are distractors, not spare answers to use in the calculation. Children circle the result and join its two inputs. Start with counters for every number; extend by asking whether another valid statement is possible. The puzzle pack supplies ready-made rounds.

2. Fizz counting — Classes 2 to 5

Count around a circle, replacing each multiple of 5 with “Fizz”: 1, 2, 3, 4, Fizz, 6, 7, 8, 9, Fizz. Use a shared number line so children can check the sequence. If someone says 15, pause and ask the group why it should be Fizz, then continue. Let a child point to the next number or pass a card instead of speaking. Change the chosen table only after the rule is secure. This practises counting patterns and recognising multiples, not division with remainders.

3. Tables bingo — Classes 2 to 5

Children put nine different answers from the chosen table into a 3 by 3 grid. For the 5 table, they could choose from 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45 and 50. Call calculations such as 5 × 6, rather than the answers; children mark 30 if they have it. Check a completed line by asking for the corresponding facts. For an easier round, allow an array or table chart. Extend with mixed tables already taught, without requiring unfamiliar facts.

4. Odd or even? — Classes 2 to 5

Call a number and have children show an “odd” or “even” card. Standing or sitting is an optional alternative, never a requirement. Ask children to pair counters: 14 makes seven pairs with none left, whereas 15 leaves one unpaired counter. Use single-digit numbers first and later include larger numbers. Children can inspect the ones digit, but should explain why pairing still works. Finish with two odd numbers, such as 7 and 9: together they make 16, an even number.

5. Human number line — Classes 1 to 5

Give a small group number cards and ask them to order themselves. The rest of the class checks. Use 12, 7, 19 and 10 for an early round: 7, 10, 12, 19 is ascending order. Put the same cards on a desk if moving is difficult or space is limited. Later try 405, 450, 504 and 540. Ask which digit settles each comparison. Equal spacing is not necessary for this ordering game; use a drawn, scaled line when distance between numbers matters.

6. Reach 21 together — Classes 1 to 3

Partners count from 1, saying either one or two successive numbers on each turn. Their shared aim is to reach 21 without skipping, repeating or going beyond it. One child might say “1, 2”, the next “3”, and the next “4, 5”. Keep a counter on a number track to record the last number spoken. Begin with a target of 10. Ask how many numbers remain after 17: four. This is a cooperative counting game, with no child made the loser for saying 21.

7. Largest number grid — Classes 3 to 5

Draw four labelled places: thousands, hundreds, tens and ones. Reveal 3, 8, 0 and 5 one at a time; children place each digit in an empty box and cannot move it during that round. Compare their results. Once all digits are known, the largest four-digit number is 8,530 and the smallest four-digit number is 3,058. Zero cannot lead a four-digit number. Use two places for an easier start. Discuss how knowing all the digits changes the choices, rather than treating an unlucky placement as poor understanding.

8. Fraction match — Classes 2 to 5

Make pairs of cards: a fraction and a picture of that fraction. For 3/4, shade three of four equal parts. Partners turn over two cards, explain whether they match and keep a matching pair. Make the whole the same size when comparing pictures. Begin with halves; introduce quarters and other fractions when taught. Older children can match 1/2 with 2/4 and explain the equivalence. Avoid pictures with unequal parts: three shaded pieces do not automatically mean three quarters.

9. Kirana shop — Classes 1 to 5

Set up a pretend shop with paper price labels and money cards, not real purchases. A pencil costs ₹8 and a notebook ₹20. Buying both costs ₹28; paying ₹50 gives ₹22 change. Partners swap shopkeeper and customer roles and record the calculation. Use whole-rupee amounts first. When decimal money is familiar, try ₹25.50 + ₹14.75 = ₹40.25. Explain that ₹1 equals 100 paise. Give children a price list to read or point to, so remembering prices does not become the hidden task.

10. Paper hive challenge — Classes 1 to 5

Pairs make an addition hive for another pair. For 7, 2, 6, 17 and 1, the intended statement is 6 + 1 = 7. Before swapping, the makers check every possible pair of input cells and keep a small answer slip. If more than one statement works, label it a multiple-answer puzzle rather than insisting on a single answer. Older children can make multiplication hives. Use the instructions in the pack to keep the rule clear and the challenge fair.

11. Two-dice board game — Classes 1 to 3

Use a simple numbered track, or a familiar snakes-and-ladders board with its agreed rules. Throw two dice, add the numbers and move that many spaces. A 4 and a 3 give seven spaces. Ask children to count on from the larger number instead of counting both dice from 1. Make a desk version with drawn dice if needed. For subtraction practice, use the difference between the larger and smaller throws, stating that rule before starting. Do not change the operation halfway through a round.

12. Cricket scoreboard — Classes 3 to 5

Give teams a pretend scorecard for four overs, with six legal balls per over: 24 legal balls altogether. Use only the runs listed on your cards and exclude extras for this maths version. Suppose the over totals are 8, 12, 6 and 10; the total is 36 runs. Children can add 8 + 12 and 6 + 10 to make 20 + 16. No knowledge of cricket is needed once the rules are explained. Extend by comparing scores or finding how many more runs would reach 50: 14.

13. Number chains — Classes 2 to 5

Write a starting number and a sequence of operations. From 10, use + 5, × 2 and − 8, in that order: 15, 30, 22. Children pass a card along, recording each intermediate result. Partners check the chain backwards using inverse operations: 22 + 8 = 30, 30 ÷ 2 = 15, 15 − 5 = 10. Start with addition alone. This is an operation chain, not a word game; a written trail helps children locate the exact step that needs correction.

14. Rangoli symmetry — Classes 1 to 5

Draw a simple dot grid with a vertical line down the middle. One partner draws a shape on the left; the other reflects it on the right, keeping corresponding points the same distance from the line. Use a square or triangle first. Fold a scrap-paper version to check that the halves coincide. Explain that not every rangoli design is symmetrical, and a repeating pattern need not be a reflection. Extend by testing a second possible line of symmetry rather than adding decoration without a mathematical purpose.

15. Bundle exchange — Classes 1 to 3

Use loose sticks and bundles of ten. Give a pair 24 sticks and ask them to represent 24 as two tens and four ones. Add eight loose sticks: the 12 ones can be exchanged for one ten and two ones, making 32. Children record 24 + 8 = 32 beside the exchange. Begin with making one bundle; extend to hundreds when appropriate. Keep bundles visible and easy to open. The exchange should reveal place value, not become a trick performed only by the teacher.

Indie asks, “What did your move show?” A child who explains an exchange, a pair or an array has given you useful evidence of learning. Two pencils cost ₹16. The bill is ₹16 + ₹20 = ₹36, so the change is ₹50 − ₹36 = ₹14. Check: ₹36 + ₹14 = ₹50.
