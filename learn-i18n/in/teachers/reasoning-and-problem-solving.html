“How do you know?” is a small question with a big place in a primary maths lesson. A correct answer tells you one thing. A child's counters, picture or explanation can show whether they understand why it works.

Reasoning need not mean a long written paragraph. In Class 1, a child may move two groups of counters together. In Class 3, they may sketch a bar. In Class 5, they may use a related fact to justify an answer. Build the language alongside the mathematics, in the language children can use confidently.

Five questions worth keeping ready

  • How do you know? Invite a model, a calculation or a known fact.
  • Is that always true? Test a statement and look for a reason or a counterexample.
  • Where is the mistake? Give an anonymous worked example and ask children to repair it.
  • Can you find another way? Compare methods without declaring that the fastest speaker has the best one.
  • Can you convince your partner? Ask the listener to check the explanation, then swap roles.
After asking a question, give quiet thinking time. Let children show a partner their drawing before speaking to the class. This gives you more evidence than taking the first raised hand.

Begin with something children can show

For Classes 1–2, put out eight counters and five more. Ask, “How many altogether? How can we check?” One child counts all 13. Another moves two from the group of five to complete ten, leaving three: 8 + 5 = 10 + 3 = 13. Both methods can be shown and discussed.

Don't take the counters away as soon as a child has given the answer. Ask them to connect the objects to the number sentence. The important step is understanding what each number represents.

One amount, several possibilities

“Make ₹50 in different ways” works well with paper money cards. Children might use ₹20 + ₹20 + ₹10, five ₹10 cards, or ten ₹5 cards. Check the three totals: 20 + 20 + 10 = 50; 5 × 10 = 50; 10 × 5 = 50.

Ask what stayed the same and what changed. The amount is unchanged; the number and value of the cards differ. For younger children, offer only two denominations. For older children, ask for an organised list using a stated set of cards, and be clear whether repeated cards are allowed.

A kirana-shop context is useful only if the maths is clear. Prices in a task are made-up examples, not claims about what goods cost locally. Children do not need to share their family's spending.

Draw the relationship before choosing an operation

For Classes 4–5, try: “One group has three times as many counters as another. Together they have 48. How many are in each group?” Draw one equal part for the smaller group and three equal parts for the larger group.

There are four equal parts altogether. 48 ÷ 4 = 12, so the smaller group has 12 and the larger has 3 × 12 = 36. Check both conditions: 12 + 36 = 48, and 36 is three times 12.

The word “together” alone is not enough to solve this problem. Children first need to understand the relationship between the groups. A bar model is a thinking aid, not a compulsory format for every question.

A familiar written solution

Some schools ask children to organise a word problem as Given, To find, Solution, Answer. Use that structure if it helps, but don't let copying headings replace understanding.

Example: “Rice costs ₹60 per kg in this problem. A customer buys 3 kg and pays ₹200. What change should the customer receive?”

  • Given: price ₹60 per kg, quantity 3 kg, payment ₹200.
  • To find: the change after paying for the rice.
  • Solution: cost = 3 × ₹60 = ₹180; change = ₹200 − ₹180 = ₹20.
  • Answer: the customer receives ₹20 change.

Check by adding the cost and change: ₹180 + ₹20 = ₹200. Also check the multiplication as 60 + 60 + 60 = 180. The unit matters: ₹180 is a cost, while 3 kg is a quantity.

Make mistakes safe to discuss

Write: “52 − 27 = 35. I subtracted the smaller digit from the larger digit in each column.” Ask what the method misses. The digits have place values; subtraction is not a choice to reverse each column.

Borrow one ten, so 52 becomes four tens and 12 ones (newer books call this regrouping). Then 12 − 7 = 5 ones, and 4 − 2 = 2 tens, giving 25. Check: 27 + 25 = 52. Another route is 52 − 20 = 32, then 32 − 7 = 25.

Use a prepared example rather than naming the child who made the mistake. Ask children to repair the reasoning, not merely cross out the answer.

“Always” needs more than one example

“Adding 10 always changes only the tens digit” sounds plausible after 24 + 10 = 34. But 90 + 10 = 100 changes the hundreds digit too. One counterexample is enough to show that the original statement is not always true.

Ask the class to improve the statement: adding 10 adds one ten, and regrouping may change other digits. Keep numbers within the range the class has learnt. Children can explain the change with bundles of ten instead of memorising an exception.

Notice the quality of the explanation

Listen for a connection between the question, the model and the calculation. Can the child identify what is known? Does each step answer part of the problem? Have they checked the result against the original conditions?

Offer a sentence beginning such as “I know this because…” or “I checked by…”. Accept pointing, drawing and oral explanation while written language develops. An incorrect answer with a thoughtful model gives you a useful teaching starting point.

Does your answer fit every part of the question? I like a second check that uses a different method!

Arrange each odd number as pairs with one counter left over. When you combine the two groups, the two leftover counters make another pair. Every counter is now paired, so the total is even. For example, 5 + 7 = 12; the pairing explains why it works beyond that one example.
