Helping with maths does not mean sitting beside your child and supplying each answer. Your most useful role is to make space for an attempt, listen to their thinking and notice where help is needed. You can do this even when the method in the maths copy looks different from the one you remember.

Start with the school diary, the current textbook page and a recent worked example. Find out what the teacher wants practised. A worksheet on a different topic may add work without helping with the question your child is trying to understand.

Make a manageable routine

Choose a time when your child is not hungry or exhausted. Keep pencils, the maths copy and any needed counters within reach. A clear patch of table or floor is enough; practice does not need a separate study room or purchased kit.

Begin with one question your child recognises, then a small amount of current work. Agree on a stopping point rather than adding more questions every time they succeed. Leave room for rest, play and other schoolwork. If a short practice session becomes a daily struggle, speak with the teacher about the workload and the underlying difficulty.

During the summer vacation, return to a few useful ideas such as counting, number bonds, tables or time. Choose a small section of a worksheet and discuss it. A large holiday pack is not automatically better practice, especially if errors are being repeated without feedback.

Let your child attempt the question first

Read the instruction together if needed, then let your child choose a starting point. Ask whether they want to use objects, draw a picture or write rough work. Keep that working visible: crossing out one attempt neatly is more informative than erasing every trace of it.

  • “What is the question asking us to find?”
  • “What do we already know?”
  • “Can you show that amount with counters or a drawing?”
  • “Does your answer fit the situation?”

Wait after asking. A pause is not proof that your child does not know. Avoid giving several hints at once, as it can become difficult to remember the question and process all the suggestions.

Notice a specific action: “You checked the tens column,” or “Your picture shows equal groups.” This tells your child which habit helped, rather than making praise depend only on a correct answer.

Follow the school's written method

If the teacher has introduced a method, use the example in the textbook or maths copy. Your child is learning both the mathematical idea and a way to record it. Showing a competing layout during homework can make that harder.

For 27 + 18, the ones make 7 + 8 = 15 ones. Exchange ten of those ones for one ten. There are now four tens and five ones, so the answer is 45. If the school calls the exchange “carry over”, connect the small carried digit to the ten being moved, not to an unexplained rule.

For 52 − 26, exchange one of the five tens for ten ones. The number is still 52, now shown as four tens and 12 ones. Subtract six ones to leave six, then two tens to leave two tens. The answer is 26. When the school says “borrow”, help your child see that the total value has not changed.

Older children may meet a written division layout. With 157 ÷ 5, the quotient is 31 and the remainder is 2. Check it using 5 × 31 + 2 = 157. The remainder is smaller than the divisor, 5. Use the steps taught at school rather than insisting on one particular long-division layout.

When your child gets stuck

Find the smallest point of confusion. Is it the instruction, the meaning of a word, a number fact, the place value or the written layout? These call for different kinds of help. More questions of the same kind will not necessarily clear up the misunderstanding.

If your child adds in a sharing problem, act out a smaller version. Share six counters equally between two people, then connect the action to division. If the operation is right but a table fact is missing, work out that fact with an array and return to the original question.

Try a smaller number without changing the idea. Before 84 ÷ 4, discuss 12 ÷ 4 with counters. Then ask what is similar in the larger question. If the smaller example also causes difficulty, stop at that foundation rather than pushing through the bigger calculation.

Write a short note for the teacher when necessary: “She can make equal groups with counters but is unsure how to write the division.” This is more useful than “She is weak in maths.” Record what your child can do as well as what is not understood yet.

Use a familiar language to explain

Your child may understand a situation in a home language before recognising the textbook wording in the teaching medium. Let them explain in that familiar language. Then connect the explanation to the school term and notation.

For a word problem, discuss the story before asking for a number sentence. Identify what is known and what must be found. Do not teach a rule that every question containing “more” means addition: “How many more?” can require comparing two amounts by subtraction.

Talk with the teacher and coordinate tuition

At a PTM, take one or two examples from the maths copy. Ask which foundation needs attention and how the teacher wants it practised. Find out whether your child can explain the idea orally, with objects or in a small group, even if the written answer is incomplete.

If your child attends tuition, share the current school method and topic with the tutor. Tuition can be useful when it addresses a clear need, but extra pages and a second method may create confusion. Agree on a consistent approach and leave time for the child to rest.

Avoid comparing marks or speed with siblings, cousins or classmates. “Not yet” leaves room to learn; a permanent label does not. Indie the honeybee sometimes needs to rearrange her counters before she sees the pattern. She is still doing mathematical work while she thinks.

Finish with a clear next step

Ask your child to explain one thing that became clearer. Keep the next practice small: one number bond, one table pattern or one place-value exchange. If worry is getting in the way, visit our guide to maths anxiety. For unfamiliar methods, read how maths is taught.

Choose practice from the worksheet collection only after identifying the skill. A completed page matters less than whether your child understands what they have done.
