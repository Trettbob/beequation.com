Your child may use bead strings, bundles of sticks, drawings or a number line before writing a calculation in columns. These are ways to make the meaning visible. The aim is not to keep children counting objects for every answer, but to help them understand what a written method represents.

Schools across India follow different boards, textbooks and teaching mediums. Topic order and recording methods can vary. Look at the worked example in your child's current textbook and maths copy before helping with homework. These explanations show the underlying ideas, not one compulsory layout for every school.

The national framework and your child's classroom

The National Education Policy describes a 5+3+3+4 structure. The Foundational Stage includes preschool and Classes 1–2; the Preparatory Stage includes Classes 3–5. Most children start Class 1 at about 6, though the exact age and cut-off date depend on your state.

The National Curriculum Framework for the Foundational Stage emphasises play, conversation and learning through activity. It says formal tests and exams are not the right tool at this stage: teachers should watch children at work and look at what they make and write. The school education framework also includes observation and short written assessments in Classes 3–5. A recommendation does not mean every school already follows identical classroom practices.

The national NIPUN Bharat mission aims for children to read and write numbers up to about 9,999 and to add, subtract and begin to multiply confidently by the end of Class 3. These mission goals and the sequence in a particular textbook are not interchangeable lists of chapters.

NCERT's newer primary books include Joyful Mathematics for Classes 1–2 and Maths Mela for Classes 3–5. They came out between 2023 and 2025, so all five classes now have a new NCERT book. Ask which book and syllabus your school is using rather than assuming a child in another school has covered the same material.

Objects, pictures and symbols

A teacher might first ask children to make 34 using three bundles of ten and four loose sticks. Next, children draw tens and ones. Finally, they write 34 and explain that the 3 represents three tens. The number stays the same across the different representations.

An abacus or place-value chart can show the same idea. Keep the column labels clear: three beads in the tens column mean 30, not three ones. Beads, sticks and base-ten blocks are useful only when the child can connect them to the quantities they represent.

A number line helps children see order, compare numbers and show jumps. For 28 + 7, jump two to 30 and five more to 35. The split is useful because 2 + 5 = 7. Children should explain the jumps rather than copy an unexplained picture.

Ask your child to connect two representations: “Where can we see that ten in your drawing and in the written sum?” This checks understanding without introducing a different homework method.

Addition and carrying over

Consider 347 + 285. Write the numbers one below the other, with hundreds, tens and ones lined up under H, T and O. Begin with the ones: 7 + 5 = 12 ones. Write 2 in the ones and carry 1 ten over to the tens.

The tens now total 4 + 8 + 1 = 13 tens. Write 3 in the tens and carry 1 hundred over to the hundreds. The hundreds total 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 hundreds. The answer is 632.

The small carried digits, written above the next column, record those tens and hundreds. The new NCERT books call this “regrouping”; parents usually say “carry over”, and in Hindi it is हासिल. Check the result in a different way: 347 + 200 = 547, then +80 = 627, then +5 = 632. Both routes give the same total.

Subtraction and borrowing

For 532 − 178, there are only two ones in the starting representation. Borrow one ten and change it into ten ones, giving 12 ones and two tens. Subtract eight ones to leave four.

Two tens are not enough to subtract seven tens. Borrow one hundred and change it into ten tens, leaving four hundreds and 12 tens. Subtract seven tens to leave five tens, then one hundred to leave three hundreds. The answer is 354.

Borrowing hasn't changed 532: four hundreds, 12 tens and 12 ones still total 532. Check by adding back: 354 + 178 = 532. This explains “borrow” (उधार in Hindi; newer books say “regroup”) as moving value between columns, not as taking a digit from nowhere.

Multiplication as equal groups

Before a written multiplication method, children need to recognise equal groups. Three rows of four counters contain 12 counters. This can be written 3 × 4 = 12. Turning the array shows four rows of three, which also make 12.

For 234 × 23, split 23 into 20 and 3. Multiply 234 by 20 to get 4,680, and by 3 to get 702. Add the partial products: 4,680 + 702 = 5,382.

A school may record these products in an expanded layout or a compact written method. In the compact method, children write a 0 in the ones place of the second row (some teachers use a cross) before multiplying by the 2 tens, because that row means × 20, not × 2. Follow the teacher's layout while asking your child to explain each line's value.

Division as sharing and grouping

Sharing 12 counters equally among three children gives four each. Making groups of three from 12 counters gives four groups. Both lead to 12 ÷ 3 = 4, but the story tells you what the four represents.

For 857 ÷ 4, one possible route removes useful groups: 4 × 200 = 800, leaving 57; 4 × 10 = 40, leaving 17; and 4 × 4 = 16, leaving 1. Add the partial quotients: 200 + 10 + 4 = 214. The quotient is 214 and the remainder is 1 (Q = 214, R = 1).

Check using dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder: 4 × 214 + 1 = 857. The remainder, 1, is smaller than 4. Whether a remainder means something left over or requires another group depends on the word problem.

The new NCERT books build division up from these partial quotients, written as 4 ) 857 ( 200 + 10 + 4, with the divisor on the left and the quotient on the right. The traditional long division layout, 4 ) 857 ( 214, works the same way, one digit at a time: divide, multiply, subtract, bring down. Some books write the quotient above the dividend instead; the steps are the same. Follow the layout in your child's copy, and don't treat the method you learnt as the only valid one.

Mental maths is reasoning, not a race

Children can use known facts: 8 + 7 is 8 + 2 + 5 = 15, and 6 × 7 can be worked out as 5 × 7 + 7 = 42. Talk about why the split is helpful. As facts become familiar, children need fewer counting steps.

“Fill in the blanks” questions can check relationships rather than recall alone. In 36 + □ = 50, the missing number is 14. Count on or use subtraction, then check 36 + 14 = 50.

What to ask at a PTM

Ask which method is currently being taught, what your child can explain and which foundation needs practice. Bring a question that caused confusion. Check whether the difficulty is with the maths, the instruction's language or the way the answer must be recorded.

Indie the honeybee shows her groups before she writes her answer. Let your child do the same when the symbols feel unfamiliar. Explore mental strategies and helping at home for more ways to support the school's work.
