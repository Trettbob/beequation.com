A school diary may mention a unit test, periodic assessment, half-yearly exam or annual exam. The names and arrangements differ between schools. In Classes 1 to 5, begin with the notice from your child's own school rather than another family's experience.

There are no board exams in primary school: the first board exams come in Class 10. Maths tests in Classes 1 to 5 are set and marked by the school. A school may use a written paper, oral questions, classroom activities or a combination. Find out what is being assessed, how the child will respond and how the teacher will use the result.

Assessment happens during ordinary lessons too

A teacher learns about a child's understanding while listening to an explanation, checking the maths copy or watching children make equal groups. A wrong written answer may sit alongside a correct spoken explanation. Both are useful evidence when deciding what to teach next.

  • Observation: how a child counts, sorts, measures or uses materials.
  • Oral questions: what a child can explain or work out in conversation.
  • Classwork: drawings, calculations, rough work and corrections in the maths copy.
  • Short written checks: a small set of questions on a particular idea.
  • School exams: a wider set of topics under the school's stated arrangements.

No single form shows everything. A child may need help reading an instruction, organising written work or recognising a fact, even when they understand the situation. Ask the teacher what the evidence suggests, rather than drawing conclusions from a total alone.

Classes 1 and 2

The National Curriculum Framework for the Foundational Stage recommends assessment through observation and children's work. It considers formal tests and examinations inappropriate at this stage. This is national framework guidance; it does not mean every school has already stopped using written tests.

For these classes, useful questions include whether a child can count a small collection accurately, make tens and ones, compare quantities or explain a picture. A conversation with objects can show understanding that a hurried written response misses.

If the school sets a test, ask about its topics, format and purpose. Keep revision close to familiar classroom work. Do not introduce a large exam-preparation routine because the notice uses the word “assessment”.

Classes 3 to 5

The school education framework includes classroom observation and short written assessments in the Preparatory Stage. Schools may also organise unit or periodic tests, half-yearly papers and annual examinations. Their timing, syllabus and marking arrangements are not identical across boards and states.

A paper may include fill in the blanks, calculations, word problems, shape questions or reading a graph. Ask whether rough work should be shown and what units are expected. For a money problem, an answer of 15 needs the context: ₹15, 15 coins or 15 items are different answers.

When preparing, practise the question formats your child has actually met. Reading a question carefully and checking the answer are useful habits. There is no need to make every home session timed unless the teacher has identified timing as a specific issue to address.

Class 5 annual exams and re-exams

In some states and schools, children who don't pass the Class 5 annual exam get a second chance through a re-exam. The applicable arrangements depend on the state, school and rules in force.

Don't assume either automatic promotion or detention from a general statement online. If this is relevant to your child, ask the school for its current written policy, the support provided before any re-exam and the dates and syllabus that apply. A relative's account of another school is not enough to establish your child's position.

Discuss any difficulty early, while there is time to work on the missing foundation. Keep the conversation about support and learning rather than threats about failing a class.

Report cards and the Holistic Progress Card

A report card may use marks, grades, descriptive comments or a mixture. Read the comments beside the total. “Needs to check place value” points to a different next step from “Needs help understanding word problems”. If a comment is unclear, ask for an example.

Some schools now use a Holistic Progress Card. It can bring together a broader picture of a child's learning rather than relying only on an exam total. Do not assume the same format or implementation in every school; ask your child's teacher how their card is prepared and interpreted.

At a PTM, ask, “What can my child explain confidently, and which one skill should we practise next?” This usually gives a more useful home plan than asking how to raise the total without identifying the difficulty.

What is PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan?

PARAKH is a national assessment body within NCERT. PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 took place on 4 December 2024 for selected children in Classes 3, 6 and 9. The Class 3 assessment covered language and mathematics.

This is a system-level survey involving selected schools and children, not a personal pass-or-fail school exam for every Class 3 child. Do not treat participation as a separate requirement to buy coaching or prepare an additional revision syllabus.

If your school communicates about a survey, read that notice for the specific arrangements. A past survey date does not establish the date or format of a future exercise. Your child's ordinary classroom learning remains the useful starting point.

Optional exams some families choose

A few exams in these years are optional and only for families who choose them. In Class 5, some children sit the Jawahar Navodaya Vidyalaya Selection Test (JNVST) for a Class 6 place at a Navodaya school, or the entrance exam for Sainik Schools; both include arithmetic. Many schools also offer maths Olympiads run by private organisations, which families pay for. None of these is part of the syllabus, and not taking them is not a disadvantage in ordinary school work.

Use a returned paper to find the next step

Look at a few questions together after your child has had time to settle. Sort the errors by what happened: misunderstood instruction, wrong operation, forgotten fact, place-value error or missing unit. These are more useful categories than “careless mistakes” for everything.

For example, if 46 + 27 is written as 63, check the carrying: six ones plus seven ones make 13 ones. Keep three ones and carry one ten. Four tens plus two tens plus that extra ten make seven tens. The correct answer is 73.

If the child understands that correction, try one related question later. If not, return to tens and ones with objects. Keep the original paper as evidence for the teacher rather than asking your child to rewrite an entire corrected paper without discussion.

Before and after a test

Beforehand, check the school diary, organise the required materials and revise a small selection of familiar questions. Keep normal sleep, meals and play in the routine. Last-minute piles of new work may not address the actual gap.

Afterwards, ask how the experience felt before asking for marks. Indie the honeybee wants to explain the question she understood and the one she wants to revisit. Let your child do both. Avoid comparisons with ranks, cousins or classmates.

For practical revision, read helping at home. If worry is affecting attempts, explore when maths feels worrying. The aim is to use assessment to guide teaching and support, not to turn one result into a label for the child.
