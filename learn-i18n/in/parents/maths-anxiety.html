Some children freeze when a maths question appears, even if they have solved something similar before. Others say “I can't do maths”, avoid opening the maths copy or worry about marks before a test. A child may describe a tight tummy or feel upset during homework.

These signs do not tell you a diagnosis or a single cause. The work may be unfamiliar, an instruction may be confusing, or the child may be worried about making a mistake. Start by listening and noticing when the difficulty occurs, rather than deciding that your child is careless or weak in maths.

Find out what feels difficult

Choose a calm moment outside homework. Ask, “Which part of maths feels worrying?” A child may mention reading aloud, answering in front of others, remembering tables, finishing a page or seeing a red correction. Their answer can help you choose a useful change.

Notice whether the worry appears with every activity or with a particular situation. Can your child share counters comfortably but hesitate over a written word problem? Can they explain orally but struggle to record an answer? Share these observations with the teacher without treating them as proof of a condition.

If your child uses a different home language from the teaching medium, let them explain a familiar situation in that language first. Then connect it to the school wording. Not recognising a mathematical word is different from not understanding the idea.

Take permanent labels out of the conversation

Avoid “You are weak in maths”, “You never understand” or “Our family is not good at maths.” These descriptions can make a difficulty sound fixed. Name the skill instead: “We are still learning how to carry a ten,” or “This table fact is not familiar yet.”

You do not need to pretend to know every answer yourself. You can say, “I don't remember this method. Let's look at your teacher's example.” That shows how an adult handles uncertainty without making the child responsible for it.

Keep comparisons with siblings, cousins and classmates out of practice. Another child's marks do not explain what your child needs next. If relatives ask about ranks, you can bring the conversation back to something specific the child is learning.

Replace “This is nothing; why can't you do it?” with “Which part shall we look at together?” A question can feel manageable to an adult and still contain several new steps for a child.

Make the first step small enough to attempt

Start with an idea your child can show, not a page full of difficult questions. Let them choose counters, a drawing or spoken reasoning. If they can solve 8 + 2, use that known fact to explore 8 + 5: add two to reach ten, then three more to make 13.

For a child who is uncertain about multiplication, arrange two rows of four counters. Count the eight counters, then connect the picture to 2 × 4 = 8. Add one more row of four to make 12. There is room to reason without recalling the fact immediately.

Agree on a stopping point. Completing two questions with understanding can be a useful session. Avoid turning success into an unexpected extra page, which can make the agreed finish feel unreliable.

Keep practice low stakes

Play a matching game with numbers and small dot pictures. Make a pretend shop, spot shapes around the room or take turns continuing a number pattern. Choose activities that do not depend on finishing first or beating someone else's score.

Let your child ask you a question too. If you make a deliberate mistake for them to investigate, say so and keep it light: “I have put a mistake in this picture. Can you find it?” Don't pretend that their own mistake is a trap or a joke.

Indie the honeybee sometimes counts a flower twice. She moves each counted flower to a separate group and checks again. You can use her example to discuss a helpful checking habit, without presenting errors as embarrassing.

Games are an option, not a reward that must be earned by finishing homework. If a game also feels stressful, stop and choose a different activity. Your child does not have to demonstrate confidence on demand.

Respond to mistakes without a rush

Look at the working before the final answer. Ask your child to explain one step. If 27 + 18 has been written as 35, the child may have added the ones correctly but forgotten the ten carried over. Revisit carrying with bundles and sticks instead of repeating the entire calculation more loudly.

Give one correction at a time. You might say, “Seven ones and eight ones make 15 ones. How can we show those as tens and ones?” Once the child sees one ten and five ones, return to the tens column. The correct total is 45.

If frustration is rising, pause the maths discussion. A break is not a punishment or proof of failure. Later, decide whether to try a smaller example or take the question to the teacher.

Work with the school

Use the school diary or a PTM to describe the situation plainly: “He explains sharing with objects, but becomes upset when the same question is written.” Ask what the teacher sees in class and whether the instruction, workload or recording method needs attention.

Discuss a realistic practice plan with one clear focus. If your child attends tuition, include the tutor in that plan. Several adults setting separate revision sheets can make a worried child feel that maths never ends. Coordinate the work rather than assuming more time will solve every difficulty.

Before a test, ask the teacher which topics and question formats to revise. Use familiar examples and leave time for sleep, meals and play. Avoid repeated conversations about possible marks or consequences.

When to seek further support

If distress continues, your child regularly avoids schoolwork or difficulty with number ideas persists despite suitable teaching, talk with the teacher about further support. A school counsellor or special educator, where available, may help identify appropriate next steps.

Dyscalculia is a learning difficulty involving number and mathematical understanding. Low marks, slow calculation or one worrying homework session do not establish it. Assessment belongs with an appropriately qualified professional, not an online checklist or a parent's guess. A child can receive teaching support while the adults investigate their needs.

The immediate goal is a safe next attempt, not a promise that every worry will disappear. Read helping at home for practical routines or school maths tests to understand assessment. Keep your child's explanation at the centre of the conversation.
