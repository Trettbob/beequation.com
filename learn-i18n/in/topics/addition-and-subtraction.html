Addition and subtraction describe more than joining and taking away. We add to find a total or an unknown part. We subtract to find what remains, what is missing or the difference between two quantities. Helping children recognise the situation is as important as helping them calculate.

Use counters, a number line and a place-value chart alongside written work. Ask your child or pupils to explain what the answer represents: pencils, rupees, metres or something else.

How learning develops

  • Class 1: join and separate small collections, tell number stories and connect them to symbols.
  • Class 2: count on, use number bonds and explore tens and ones in addition and subtraction.
  • Class 3: use place value with three-digit calculations and explain regrouping.
  • Class 4: calculate with larger numbers and solve problems involving money and measurement.
  • Class 5: choose methods, estimate results and check multi-step problems within the textbook's number range.

The precise sequence depends on the school book. Keep the representation familiar while changing the size of the numbers.

Start with the meaning

Joining and taking away

There are 8 pencils in a pot and 5 beside it. Put them together to show 8 + 5 = 13. Then start with 13 pencils and remove 5 to show 13 − 5 = 8. The same objects explain how the operations are connected.

Ask the child to invent a matching story. It need not copy the wording of the homework question. The important thing is that the quantities and action agree with the calculation.

Finding the difference

One pupil has 13 counters and another has 8. Match the counters in pairs. Five are left unmatched, so the difference is 5. This gives 13 − 8 = 5 without taking anything away from either collection.

On a number line, count from 8 up to 13. This is useful when the two numbers are close. For example, the difference between 98 and 103 is 5: move 2 to reach 100, then 3 to reach 103.

Mental strategies children can explain

Make ten

For 8 + 5, split 5 into 2 and 3. Add 2 to make 10, then add the remaining 3:

8 + 5 = 8 + 2 + 3 = 13.

Use a ten-frame or two rows of five spaces to show why the split helps. Children need to know which pairs make ten, such as 6 and 4 or 7 and 3.

Partition and adjust

For 46 + 23, add 20 and then 3: 46 + 20 = 66, and 66 + 3 = 69. For 49 + 26, add 50 + 26 = 76, then subtract the extra 1 to get 75.

Ask which method suits the numbers. A child may count on for a small addition, adjust near a multiple of ten, or use columns when many place-value parts are involved.

Ask “What did you change, and how did you keep the value the same?” This question makes partitioning and adjustment visible.

Column addition: regrouping explained

Work out 347 + 285. Align ones under ones, tens under tens and hundreds under hundreds.

  1. Ones: 7 + 5 = 12 ones. Regroup these as 1 ten and 2 ones.
  2. Tens: 4 tens + 8 tens + the regrouped ten = 13 tens. Regroup as 1 hundred and 3 tens.
  3. Hundreds: 3 hundreds + 2 hundreds + the regrouped hundred = 6 hundreds.

The answer is 632. An estimate, 350 + 280 = 630, suggests that 632 is sensible. The small digits recorded above a column represent regrouped units, not numbers that appear without a reason.

Column subtraction: exchange without changing the total

For 532 − 178, begin with 5 hundreds, 3 tens and 2 ones.

  1. Exchange 1 ten for 10 ones. There are now 5 hundreds, 2 tens and 12 ones. Subtract 8 ones to leave 4.
  2. Exchange 1 hundred for 10 tens. There are now 4 hundreds and 12 tens. Subtract 7 tens to leave 5 tens.
  3. Subtract 1 hundred from 4 hundreds to leave 3 hundreds.

The answer is 354. Check by adding: 354 + 178 = 532.

When there is a zero

For 500 − 278, exchange 1 hundred for 10 tens, then exchange 1 of those tens for 10 ones. The starting quantity is now represented as 4 hundreds, 9 tens and 10 ones. Subtract 2 hundreds, 7 tens and 8 ones to leave 222.

Use a chart or draw the exchanges before recording them in columns. Saying that a zero “becomes nine” hides the exchange that children need to understand.

Common mistakes and useful responses

  • Subtracting the smaller digit from the larger in every column: 532 − 178 is not 446. Build 532 and exchange units where needed.
  • Misaligned digits: use squared paper or label the columns before writing.
  • Forgetting a regrouped unit: explain its value aloud and point to its column.
  • Choosing an operation from one word: draw the situation. “More” can appear in a question that needs subtraction.

Five-minute activities

  1. Hidden counters: show 10, hide some and work out the hidden part.
  2. Number-line jumps: show 27 + 15 using a jump of 10 and then 5.
  3. Compare collections: pair two groups and describe the difference.
  4. Spot the method: compare two correct solutions to 49 + 26.
  5. Check my answer: give a subtraction answer and ask pupils to test it with addition.
