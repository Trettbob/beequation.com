Multiplication finds a total when groups are equal. Division can share a total into equal groups or find how many groups of a given size can be made. Children need to see these meanings before they memorise a written method.

Use counters, seeds, bottle caps or drawn circles. Ask your child to describe what each number represents: the number of groups, the size of each group or the total. A correct calculation is more useful when the child can connect it to the question.

Multiplication and division across the classes

  • Class 1: make small equal groups in play and share objects fairly, without symbols.
  • Class 2: multiplication as repeated addition and equal groups, the × sign, and the first tables (2, 3, 4, 5 and 10 in most books); division as fair sharing.
  • Class 3: tables up to 10, multiplying by a one-digit number, the ÷ sign, division facts and remainders.
  • Class 4: multiplying by a two-digit number, long division by a one-digit number, and multiples (factors too in many books).
  • Class 5: larger numbers, long division by two-digit numbers, factors and prime numbers; HCF and LCM in many private books.

The national NIPUN Bharat targets include the tables of 2, 3 and 4 in Class 2 and the tables of 2 to 10, with division facts, by the end of Class 3. Many schools and families then go on to tables up to 20 in Classes 4 and 5. That is common practice, but understanding what the facts mean comes first.

Equal groups and arrays

Three plates with seven laddoos on each plate contain 7 + 7 + 7 = 21 laddoos. Write this as 3 × 7 = 21: three groups of seven.

An array arranges objects in equal rows and columns. Draw three rows of seven dots. Turn the paper and you can see seven rows of three dots. The total remains 21, showing that 3 × 7 = 7 × 3.

This change of order works for multiplication. It does not work in the same way for division: 21 ÷ 3 = 7, but 3 ÷ 21 is not 7. Keep the meaning of each operation visible.

Before your child multiplies, ask them to check that every group really has the same number. If one plate has fewer laddoos, a single multiplication no longer describes it; they need to add instead.

Build tables from facts your child knows

Many children learn tables by reciting them aloud: “two ones are two, two twos are four…” (in Hindi, the पहाड़ा: “दो एकम दो, दो दूनी चार”). Reciting helps memory, and tables become more meaningful when children can rebuild a forgotten fact. If your child knows 5 × 6 = 30, one more group of six gives 6 × 6 = 36. If 4 × 7 = 28, doubling the total gives 8 × 7 = 56.

Connect each new fact to an array or equal groups. Counting in twos, fives and tens is helpful, but children should also explain what they are counting. Five jumps of two reach 10 because there are five equal groups of two.

Practise a few facts at a time, mixing questions rather than reciting only in order. Allow time to think. A child who derives an answer accurately is using mathematics, even before recall becomes fluent.

Two meanings of division

Sharing: Put 24 laddoos into four boxes equally. Each box receives six, so 24 ÷ 4 = 6. Here you know the number of boxes and find the amount in each.

Grouping: Put 24 laddoos into boxes holding six each. You need four boxes, so 24 ÷ 6 = 4. Here you know the amount in each box and find the number of boxes.

Use the same objects for both questions. Ask which number changed its role. This helps children avoid treating every division question as “share between this many people”.

The related facts form a family: 4 × 6 = 24, 6 × 4 = 24, 24 ÷ 4 = 6 and 24 ÷ 6 = 4. Multiplication and division undo one another when used this way.

Multiply using place value

To calculate 234 × 23, split 23 into 20 and 3. This example suits children whose textbook includes multiplication by a two-digit number.

234 × 3 = 702
234 × 20 = 4,680
702 + 4,680 = 5,382

The second partial product is 4,680, not 468, because it is twenty groups of 234. In the column method, many teachers ask children to write a 0 (some use a cross) in the ones place of the second row before multiplying by the 2. Ask your child to say why: “this row is multiplying by twenty, not two.”

Estimate: 234 is near 230 and 23 is near 20, giving about 4,600. An exact answer of 5,382 is reasonable. An answer of 538 would be far too small: even 234 × 3 is already 702.

Divide in manageable parts

Consider 857 ÷ 4. Find useful multiples of four, then keep track of the groups removed:

4 × 200 = 800; 857 − 800 = 57.
4 × 10 = 40; 57 − 40 = 17.
4 × 4 = 16; 17 − 16 = 1.

The quotient is 200 + 10 + 4 = 214, with remainder 1. The NCERT books for Classes 4 and 5 call these steps partial quotients and write them in the familiar bracket layout, with the divisor on the left and the quotient on the right: 4 ) 857 ( 200 + 10 + 4.

Some books write the quotient above the dividend instead; the steps are the same. Keep to the layout in your child's copy, and make sure they can explain the value of every step.

Check: 4 × 214 + 1 = 856 + 1 = 857. The remainder is smaller than the divisor, 4. If four or more objects remained, another complete group could still be made.

For a division with no remainder, 9,876 ÷ 12 = 823. Check by multiplying: 823 × 12 = 8,230 + 1,646 = 9,876. Use this larger example only when it matches the book's level.

What does the remainder mean?

Twenty-six pencils packed three to a box give 26 ÷ 3: quotient 8, remainder 2. Eight boxes are full, with two pencils left over.

If the question asks for the number of full boxes, the answer is eight. If all pencils must be packed, you need nine boxes, with the last box partly filled. The calculation is the same, but the question changes the final answer.

Do not discard a remainder automatically or round up every time. Ask what is being counted and what must happen to the leftover amount.

Useful words

The product is the answer to a multiplication; in 234 × 23, 234 is the multiplicand and 23 the multiplier. In 24 ÷ 4 = 6, 24 is the dividend, 4 the divisor and 6 the quotient. A remainder is what is left after making complete equal groups. Indian books always teach the check dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder. In Hindi these are भाज्य, भाजक, भागफल and शेषफल. Introduce the words alongside a worked example, not as a list to learn without meaning.

Common mistakes and practice ideas

  • Unequal groups: build an example with one different group and discuss why a single multiplication does not describe it.
  • Lost place value: label the partial products “three groups” and “twenty groups” before adding.
  • Tables without meaning: draw the matching array for a fact your child recalls.
  • Ignoring leftovers: use counters to compare full boxes with boxes needed for everything.
  • No check: multiply the quotient by the divisor, add the remainder and compare with the dividend.

For short classroom practice, let pairs create an array, write its fact family and swap it with another pair. At home, share 12 counters in different ways and record the number and size of the groups.

26 ÷ 3 gives quotient 8 and remainder 2. Eight packets hold 24 seeds, leaving two. Indie needs one more packet for those, so she needs nine packets altogether.
