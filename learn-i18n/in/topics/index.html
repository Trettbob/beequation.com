A question about homework often starts with one small thing: why is there a zero here, what does this fraction mean, or which hand tells the minutes? Choose a topic below for explanations, worked examples and short activities you can use with your child or pupils.

These pages are written for primary maths in India. They use Classes 1 to 5, familiar classroom words and Indian money notation. Schools follow different boards and textbooks, so use the class guidance as a starting point rather than a fixed timetable.

Explore the topics

Build a sense of number with counting, bundles, place-value charts and zero. Connect objects and number lines to mental strategies and column methods. Make sense of equal groups, tables, sharing, grouping and remainders. Explore equal parts, fractions of a collection and equivalent fractions. Choose useful strategies without making speed the main goal. Compare and measure length, weight, capacity, perimeter and area. Describe flat shapes and solid objects, symmetry, turns and patterns. Read clocks and calendars, then work out how long an activity takes. Count rupees, compare prices, read a bill and check change. Organise information and read tables, pictographs and bar graphs. Find rules, explain equality and solve missing-number problems. Optional explanations for children whose school introduces these ideas.

Where should we start?

Classes 1 and 2: objects, pictures and number talk

Counting a collection accurately matters more than reciting a long number sequence. Ask your child to move each counter once, say one number for it and tell you the total. Use pencils, bottle caps or seeds that are safe for the children taking part.

Then connect the collection to a numeral and a drawing. A group of ten loose sticks can become one bundle of ten. Joining and taking away small collections helps addition and subtraction make sense. Coins, clocks and shapes give plenty of opportunities to notice maths without turning every moment into a lesson.

For Classes 1 and 2, begin with objects, pictures and short conversations before asking children to record a calculation.

Class 3: equal groups and connected ideas

Multiplication tables are more useful when children know what the facts describe. Arrange 12 counters in three rows of four and connect the picture to multiplication and division. Encourage children to explain both sharing equally and making groups of a given size.

Fractions begin with equal parts of a whole or a collection. Measurement, calendars, money and simple records of information bring these number ideas into everyday situations.

Classes 4 and 5: explain, estimate and check

Larger numbers do not mean that drawings and objects have to disappear. A place-value chart can explain regrouping; a strip of paper can explain equivalent fractions; square tiles can show why area and perimeter are different.

Children can compare methods and decide which one suits the numbers. Before calculating, ask what a sensible answer might be. Afterwards, ask how they could check it.

Start with the idea your child can explain, not the hardest question on the sheet. Change one feature at a time: the number, the picture or the wording.

A small routine that works at home or school

  1. Notice: show a collection, a shape, a clock or a short problem.
  2. Ask: “What do you know already?” Give time to think.
  3. Represent: use objects, a drawing, a number line or a chart.
  4. Explain: invite the child to describe what each number means.
  5. Check: count again, estimate or use the opposite operation.

A few thoughtful questions are enough. If an explanation is unclear, return to the objects rather than repeating the rule more loudly. In a classroom, let pupils compare two drawings before discussing which one matches the problem.

When I visit flowers, I can count them one by one or organise them into groups. Your child can try both ways too. Your child does not need to use the same method as the person beside them.

Textbooks and extra topics

The current NCERT primary maths books develop ideas through activities and problems. Other boards and school books may introduce some ideas earlier or in a different order. Follow the method and vocabulary used in your child's classroom while helping them understand why it works.

Formal decimals and percentages are not core topics in the current NCERT maths books for Classes 1 to 5. The decimal page is an optional resource, not a list of things every Class 5 child must know. The same care applies to formal ratio, large-number extensions and topics beyond the child's book.

Try a question together

There are 4 counters in each group. Share the counters one at a time, or make three groups of four. Check that 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. This also shows 3 × 4 = 12 and 12 ÷ 3 = 4.

Practice without pressure

Choose a short worksheet after talking about the idea. A correct answer is useful, but the explanation tells you more. Ask your child to draw or describe one question rather than doing many similar questions without discussion.
