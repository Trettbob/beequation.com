A fraction tells us about equal parts of a whole or equal shares of a collection. Children need time to handle, draw and compare these parts before working with symbols. Folding paper and sharing counters are often more useful than a page of rules.

Start with the work in your child's textbook. In the current NCERT primary sequence, visual halves and quarters appear before the formal terms numerator and denominator. Those terms belong to Class 5 work; there is no need to introduce all the vocabulary at once.

How fractions develop through primary school

  • Class 1: share objects fairly and talk about equal and unequal pieces in everyday situations.
  • Class 2: continue practical sharing and folding, following the language and activities in the school book.
  • Class 3: explore halves, quarters and other visible parts of a whole.
  • Class 4: compare and describe fractions with pictures, and explore equivalent parts as the textbook introduces them.
  • Class 5: connect fraction notation and vocabulary with equal parts, equivalent fractions and amounts.

Schools may use different books. Calculating with unlike denominators should be treated as an extension where it is not part of the child's current lessons, rather than a requirement for every Class 5 pupil.

The parts must be equal

Fold a rectangular sheet into two matching parts. Each part is one half, written 1/2. Fold it again to make four matching parts. Each part is one quarter, written 1/4.

Now shade three of those four parts. The shaded fraction is 3/4: three quarters. We count the equal parts, not how dark the shading is or how much space the picture takes on the page.

A roti cut into a large piece and a small piece has two pieces, but they are not halves. Ask, “How could we check whether these pieces are equal?” Children might match paper pieces or compare their areas.

Understand the two numbers

In 3/4, the denominator, 4, says that the whole has been divided into four equal parts. The numerator, 3, says how many of those parts we are considering.

Keep a picture beside the notation. Ask your child to point to the whole, then one part, then the three shaded parts. A child who can name the numbers but cannot identify the whole still needs practical work.

Indie keeps the whole in view. When she compares fractions, she checks that the original shapes or collections are the same size.

Compare fractions using the same whole

Take two identical paper strips. Divide one into three equal parts and the other into five. One third is larger than one fifth: 1/3 > 1/5. Dividing the same whole into more equal pieces makes each piece smaller.

For fractions with the same denominator, count the parts. Three quarters is greater than one quarter because three equal parts are more than one of those parts: 3/4 > 1/4.

Be careful with real food. Half a large mango may contain more fruit than half a small mango. Both are halves of their own mango, but the wholes are different. Use identical paper shapes when teaching a comparison.

Different fractions can name the same amount

Shade half a strip. Divide that same strip into four equal parts without changing the shading. Two of the four parts are shaded. Divide it into eight equal parts and four are shaded.

1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8. These are equivalent fractions. The names change, but the shaded amount does not.

Once the picture is understood, connect it to the numbers: multiplying both numerator and denominator by 2 turns 1/2 into 2/4. Multiplying both by 4 turns 1/2 into 4/8. Changing only one number changes the fraction.

Find a fraction of a collection

To find 3/4 of 20 counters, first split the counters into four equal groups. Each group contains 20 ÷ 4 = 5 counters. Take three groups: 3 × 5 = 15.

3/4 of 20 = 15. The denominator tells us the number of equal groups; the numerator tells us how many groups to take.

For one third of 12 laddoos, make three equal shares. Each share has four laddoos, so 1/3 of 12 is 4. Use counters for the explanation rather than asking children to handle food unnecessarily.

Add and subtract parts

Where the textbook introduces fraction calculations, begin with parts of the same size. One quarter plus two quarters is three quarters: 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4. The pieces are still quarters, so the denominator stays 4.

Three quarters minus one quarter leaves two quarters: 3/4 − 1/4 = 2/4 = 1/2.

For an optional extension with unlike denominators, use equivalent fractions first. To calculate 1/2 + 1/3, divide a strip into six equal parts. One half is 3/6 and one third is 2/6. Together they make 5/6.

Similarly, 3/4 − 1/2 becomes 3/4 − 2/4 = 1/4. Drawing the parts explains why a common denominator is useful; it is not a rule to memorise without meaning.

Common mistakes to discuss

  • Counting unequal parts: check equality before naming a fraction.
  • Thinking a larger denominator means a larger piece: compare thirds and fifths of identical strips.
  • Adding denominators: 1/4 + 2/4 is 3/4, not 3/8. The size of each part has not changed.
  • Losing track of the whole: name the whole explicitly before comparing pictures or collections.

Practise through making and explaining

  • Fold paper into halves and quarters, then label the parts.
  • Draw two different shapes that each show 3/4.
  • Share 12 counters between two, three and four toy plates.
  • Make a matching game with pictures and equivalent fraction cards.
  • Ask pupils to draw a convincing example and a non-example of one half.
One quarter of 24 is 24 ÷ 4 = 6. Half of 6 is 3, so Indie gives away 3 and keeps 3 counters.

Questions parents and teachers ask

Pictures and equal sharing should give the words meaning. Introduce numerator and denominator alongside the Class 5 textbook work, rather than making the names an early test. A whole can be divided into four equal parts in different ways. The important check is that each part has the same amount, not that all dividing lines look alike. Check the current school book. This page includes an optional visual extension, but it is not a compulsory expectation for every primary class.
