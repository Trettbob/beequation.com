These free worksheets give parents and teachers a small, focused way to practise an idea. Choose by topic below, or use the class lists as a starting point. A class tag is a guide, not a statement that every Indian school teaches that skill in the same term.

Each worksheet offers a printable PDF and a separate answer sheet. Accessible large-print and Braille options are available alongside the standard version. For help choosing and using these formats, see our accessible maths and Braille page. Preview the sheet before printing, especially if your child needs more writing space.

Choose by class

Match the sheet to the child's current schoolwork. Current NCERT books, state-board books and other school books do not always introduce a method or number range at the same point. If a skill is unfamiliar, teach it with objects or a drawing first.

Class 1

Start with counting, tens and ones, small addition and subtraction, shapes, money and familiar clock times. Use counters or sticks alongside the sheet when needed.

Class 2

Choose work that builds on the child's secure number range. Use equal groups to explain early tables and a pretend shop to explain change.

Class 3

Connect written addition and subtraction to place value. Explain multiplication and division facts with groups and arrays; introduce clock intervals when they match the school's sequence.

Class 4

Fractions, measures and written multiplication are useful choices when already introduced. Some tagged sheets, including decimal work, may be extensions in your school's programme.

Class 5

Choose from fraction, calculation, measurement and data work. Decimal and percentage sheets are optional additional practice: check the school textbook rather than assuming they are required for every Class 5 child.

Choose by topic

Counting and place value

Missing numbers, tens and ones, hundreds and rounding. Ask what each digit represents before asking for a rule. Read the place-value guide if regrouping needs a fresh explanation.

Addition and subtraction

Number bonds, calculations within 20 and column methods. A child who finds a column sheet difficult may need practice exchanging one ten for ten ones, rather than a longer sheet.

Multiplication and division

Early tables, multiplication grids, division facts and short multiplication. Use our tables practice guide to connect a remembered fact to its meaning.

Fractions

Shaded fractions, fractions of amounts and equivalent fractions. Make sure the whole is clear and its parts are equal before comparing answers.

Decimals and percentages: additional practice

Use tenths, hundredths and percentages only after the school has introduced them, or as an agreed extension. These sheets are not a national primary syllabus checklist.

Telling the time

Clock times from o'clock and half past to quarter hours and five-minute intervals. Let children move the hands on a teaching clock before writing the time.

Money

Counting coins and finding change in rupees and paise. Keep the total spent and the amount paid visible, then check that cost plus change equals the payment.

Measurement

Perimeter and area practice. Trace the boundary for perimeter and cover the surface with squares for area; the two answers use different kinds of units.

Shape and geometry

Identify and describe 2D shapes. Ask about sides and corners, not only whether a shape looks like the example on a classroom chart.

Data and graphs

Read bar charts using their labels and scales. Where a bar-chart sheet is ahead of the child's schoolwork, begin with sorting objects or a pictograph instead.

Missing-number problems

These sheets sit in our algebra and ratio collection, but the primary activity is finding an unknown number, such as □ + 7 = 15. It does not require formal algebra or ratio notation.

Use a sheet without turning it into a test

Try one example together. Let the child explain a second example, then decide whether independent practice will help. There is no need to finish every question in one sitting or add a timer. For a class with different starting points, choose a smaller number range or offer counters while keeping the same mathematical idea.

Check a few answers together and discuss one mistake calmly. For 46 + 19, a child might use 46 + 20 − 1 = 65. Checking 65 − 19 = 46 confirms the sum; copying “65” from an answer sheet does not tell you whether the method is understood.

Leave room for thinking. Keep scrap paper, counters or a number line beside the worksheet. Indie, our honeybee, would rather hear how a child found one answer than see a full sheet of unexplained guesses.
