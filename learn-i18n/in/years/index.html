A class number gives you a useful starting point, not a complete picture of a child's maths. Your child may be confident with tables but still need objects to understand fractions. Start with the idea they are learning now, and go back a step whenever that helps.

These guides describe what most children learn in each class, with particular care over the current NCERT books (Joyful Mathematics for Classes 1 and 2, Maths Mela for Classes 3 to 5). CBSE, ICSE and state board schools may use different books or bring in some topics earlier. Your child's textbook, copy and school diary are the best guide to this week's work. (Some schools say “Standard” instead of “Class”; the guides are the same.)

Choose your child's class

Usually ages 6–7. Counting, making small numbers, adding and taking away, shapes and everyday comparisons. Usually ages 7–8. Tens and ones, two-digit calculations, equal groups, sharing, money and measurement. Usually ages 8–9. Numbers to 1,000 (9,999 in many books), tables to 10, division and familiar fractions. Usually ages 9–10. Calculations with larger numbers, equivalent fractions, measurement, area and pictographs. Usually ages 10–11. Five-digit numbers, factors, division, fractions, area, volume and bar graphs.

How the primary years fit together

Under the 5+3+3+4 structure of NEP 2020, three years of pre-school (Nursery, LKG and UKG, or Balvatika) and Classes 1–2 form the Foundational Stage. Classes 3–5 form the Preparatory Stage. In the early years, children need plenty of chances to handle objects, talk, draw and play. Written work grows later, but practical explanations stay valuable.

The national NIPUN Bharat mission aims for children to read and write numbers up to about 9,999 and to add, subtract and begin to multiply confidently by the end of Class 3. Many primary schools set their own tests.

Class 1 is generally described as ages 6–7; check your state's current admission rules and cut-off date. The age ranges here are only a guide. Please don't use them to judge whether a child is ahead or behind.

Ask, “Can you show me how you know?” A drawing, a handful of counters or an explanation in your home language can reveal understanding that a written answer does not.

Choose a manageable next step

If a page feels too demanding, choose one earlier skill that supports it. Understanding tens and ones helps with regrouping. Equal groups help with tables and division. Sharing a whole into equal parts prepares children for fractions. A short, successful activity is more useful than finishing a long worksheet in tears.
