A class number gives you a useful starting point, not a complete picture of a child's maths. Your child may be confident with tables but still need objects to understand fractions. Start with the idea they are learning now, and go back a step whenever that helps.

These guides describe the broad progression in primary maths, with particular care over current NCERT content. State boards, other boards and individual schools may use different books or introduce some topics earlier. Your child's textbook, copy and school diary are the best guide to this week's work.

Choose your child's class

Usually ages 6–7. Counting, making small numbers, adding and taking away, shapes and everyday comparisons. Usually ages 7–8. Tens and ones, two-digit calculations, equal groups, sharing, money and measurement. Usually ages 8–9. Numbers to 1,000 in NCERT work, tables, multiplication, division and familiar fractions. Usually ages 9–10. Calculations with larger numbers, equivalent fractions, measurement, area and pictographs. Usually ages 10–11. Five-digit numbers, factors, division, fractions, area, volume and bar graphs.

How the primary years fit together

Under the 5+3+3+4 structure, preschool and Classes 1–2 form the Foundational Stage. Classes 3–5 form the Preparatory Stage. In the early years, children need plenty of opportunities to handle objects, talk, draw and play. Written work becomes more substantial later, but practical explanations remain valuable.

Most children start Class 1 at about 6, though the exact age and cut-off date depend on your state. The age ranges here are only a guide. Do not use them to judge whether a child is ahead or behind.

Ask, “Can you show me how you know?” A drawing, a handful of counters or an explanation in your home language can reveal understanding that a written answer does not.

Choose a manageable next step

If a page feels too demanding, choose one earlier skill that supports it. Understanding tens and ones helps with regrouping. Equal groups help with tables and division. Sharing a whole into equal parts prepares children for fractions. A short, successful activity is more useful than finishing a long worksheet in tears.
