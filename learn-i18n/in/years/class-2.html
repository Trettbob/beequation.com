In Class 2, children begin to use groups rather than count everything one by one. A bundle of ten, a row of counters or an equal share can help them explain a calculation. Keep these practical models available even when schoolwork looks more written.

Children are usually about 7–8. Current NCERT Class 2 number work centres on numbers to 100. The national NIPUN Bharat targets include reading and writing numbers to 999 at this stage, and some school books introduce hundreds earlier. These are different reference points, not a reason to assume every Class 2 child has the same syllabus.

Understand tens and ones

Make 46 with four bundles of ten sticks and six loose sticks. Write 46 beside them. Ask what the 4 represents: four tens, or forty, not four single sticks. Ask what changes if you add one loose stick, or another whole bundle.

Use a number line to compare numbers, fill missing numbers and explore before and after. When comparing 46 and 64, look at the tens first. Six tens are more than four tens, even though 46 has more ones.

If your child's book includes hundreds, use the same structure. In 345 there are three hundreds, four tens and five ones. Present this as an extension when it is part of the school lesson, rather than making three-digit work a requirement for every child.

Add and subtract with regrouping

Before teaching a written method, show why a ten may need to be regrouped. Ten loose ones can become one bundle of ten. Equally, one bundle of ten can be opened into ten ones. The amount stays the same.

Worked example: 36 + 27

Thirty-six is three tens and six ones. Twenty-seven is two tens and seven ones. Together there are five tens and thirteen ones. Regroup ten of those ones as another ten. Six tens and three ones make 63, so 36 + 27 = 63.

Worked example: 52 − 26

Make five tens and two ones. To remove six ones, open one ten. You now have four tens and twelve ones. Remove two tens and six ones. Two tens and six ones remain: 52 − 26 = 26. Check by adding 26 + 26 = 52.

Your school may call this carrying or borrowing. Help your child connect those words to regrouping rather than treating them as instructions without meaning. Follow the layout shown by the teacher. If three-digit columns are in your child's book, use objects or a place-value drawing to explain the extra column.

Ask your child to estimate before calculating. For 36 + 27, an answer around sixty makes sense. An answer such as 513 suggests that the tens and ones need another look.

Equal groups and tables

Arrange twelve buttons in three rows of four. There are three equal groups, each with four buttons. Write 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 and connect it to 3 × 4 = 12. The multiplication sign gives a shorter way to record equal groups.

NIPUN Bharat includes tables of 2, 3 and 4 in its Class 2 targets. Many school books also practise tables of 5 and 10. Work with the tables your child's teacher has introduced. Counting in groups, arrays and repeated addition help the facts make sense before fluent recall develops.

Turn the button arrangement to see four rows of three. It still contains twelve buttons. Ask your child what has changed and what has stayed the same. This is a useful start to understanding why changing the order of two factors does not change their product.

Share fairly

Give three toy animals twelve counters to share. Your child can give each animal one counter at a time. Each receives four. Then ask whether the shares are equal and whether all twelve counters have been used.

There is another question hidden in the same collection: how many groups of four can you make from twelve? Make the groups and count them. Both experiences prepare children for division. A formal division symbol or a written algorithm is not needed to understand fair sharing at this point.

Measure, sort and describe

Compare lengths and begin using a ruler when introduced at school. Show that measurement starts at the zero mark, not necessarily at the end of the ruler. Talk about centimetres through small familiar objects.

Use the words gram, kilogram, millilitre and litre with packets and containers. A rice packet and a water bottle give these units meaning. Do not expect children to calculate conversions before their lessons introduce them.

Describe shapes by their sides and corners. Turn a square on its corner and check that it is still a square. Tangram pieces can be arranged into different pictures while the pieces themselves stay the same. Sort objects by one feature, then try a different rule.

Money, calendars and patterns

In a pretend shop, buy something for ₹63 and pay with ₹100. Counting on from 63 to 100 gives ₹37 change: ₹7 reaches 70 and another ₹30 reaches 100. Check that ₹63 + ₹37 = ₹100.

Children may recognise notes such as ₹100 and ₹500 even when their calculation work uses smaller amounts. Explain that 100 paise make ₹1. Keep practical money work separate from later formal decimal lessons.

Use a calendar to find a date or count days until a family event. Clock work varies by school; if your child's book introduces o'clock and half past, move the hands on a model clock together. Record simple choices with tally marks or pictures and discuss which group has more. Continue repeating patterns and number patterns by explaining the rule, not guessing the next item.

Build confidence through explanation

Ask for a drawing or a spoken explanation before correcting an answer. Indie the honeybee can organise flowers in rows because she has already counted small collections carefully. If grouping is difficult for your child, revisit that earlier step. A little practical work can be more useful than another page of tables.
