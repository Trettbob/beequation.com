Class 4 maths asks children to connect skills: reading a problem, choosing an operation, carrying out a calculation and checking what the answer means. Children are usually about 9–10. They still benefit from a picture or practical model when an idea is new.

Current NCERT Maths Mela develops number work in the thousands. Some school books introduce five-digit numbers or additional fraction procedures earlier. Match practice to the textbook and the teacher's method; a longer list of topics is not automatically a better guide.

Read larger numbers and estimate

Break 4,706 into 4,000 + 700 + 6. The zero shows there are no tens. Compare it with 4,760 by looking at each place from left to right. The thousands and hundreds match; six tens make 4,760 larger than 4,706.

Rounding can help with estimates when it is part of the school lesson. For example, 2,348 is about 2,300 to the nearest hundred, and 1,627 is about 1,600. Their sum should be around 3,900. The exact answer is 3,975, which fits that estimate. An estimate is a check, not a replacement for an exact answer when one is requested.

Keep regrouping visible

Four-digit addition and subtraction use the same place-value reasoning as smaller calculations. Align the columns, including zeros. A carried ten represents ten ones; a regrouped hundred represents ten tens.

For 4,032 − 1,785, regroup carefully across the zero. The final difference is 2,247. Check by adding 1,785 + 2,247 = 4,032. If the written steps are confusing, represent the thousands, hundreds, tens and ones with a chart before repeating the calculation.

Multiply by a two-digit number

Split the multiplier into tens and ones so each part has meaning. A grid, an expanded calculation and a compact written method can all represent the same multiplication. Follow the school's layout for submitted work.

Worked example: 234 × 23

Twenty-three is twenty plus three. First calculate 234 × 3 = 702. Then calculate 234 × 20 = 4,680. Add the two products: 702 + 4,680 = 5,382. Check the scale: 234 × 20 is already 4,680, so the total must be larger than that.

Do not teach a row in a written multiplication as an unexplained instruction to “put a zero”. Explain that the calculation is with twenty, not two. Place value determines where each part belongs.

Divide in manageable parts

NCERT work uses ways of dividing through useful multiples and partial quotients. Schools may also teach a standard written division layout. Whichever layout your child uses, they should be able to explain the groups being removed.

Worked example: 857 ÷ 4

Four groups of 200 use 800, leaving 57. Four groups of 10 use 40, leaving 17. Four groups of 4 use 16, leaving 1. Add the parts of the quotient: 200 + 10 + 4 = 214. So 857 ÷ 4 = 214 remainder 1.

Check with divisor × quotient + remainder: 4 × 214 + 1 = 857. The remainder is smaller than four, so another whole group cannot be made. Some schools introduce two-digit divisors at this stage; do not assume that is the same expectation in every book.

Read the question again after calculating. Three buses with 24 children each asks for a total; 72 children shared among three buses asks for a share. The numbers alone do not tell you which operation to use.

Multiples and equivalent fractions

A multiple comes from a whole-number multiplication. The first positive multiples of six are 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30. Explore number patterns and divisibility by 2, 5 and 10 with grouped objects or the last digit. Formal prime-number work belongs later in the NCERT progression.

Fractions become easier to compare when the whole stays the same. Shade half of a paper strip, then divide the same-sized strip into four equal parts and shade two. Both show the same amount: 1/2 = 2/4.

Similarly, 2/3 = 4/6. Draw thirds and split each third in two. For a collection, a quarter of twenty is five because 20 ÷ 4 = 5. If your school introduces adding or subtracting fractions with the same denominator, use a picture: 2/7 + 3/7 = 5/7. The pieces remain sevenths; their number changes. Do not add extra decimal or improper-fraction requirements to the NCERT core on that basis.

Measurement, perimeter and area

Use familiar unit relationships in practical problems: 100 cm = 1 m, 1,000 g = 1 kg and 1,000 mL = 1 L. A length of 2 m 35 cm is 235 cm. Check that your answer uses the unit requested.

Perimeter is the distance around a boundary. A rectangle 8 cm long and 5 cm wide has perimeter 8 + 5 + 8 + 5 = 26 cm. Area describes the surface covered. On a centimetre-square grid, the same rectangle covers 8 × 5 = 40 square centimetres. Counting unit squares explains why these are different measurements.

Use bills and rates too. Three kilograms of rice at ₹40 per kilogram cost 3 × ₹40 = ₹120. At this level, begin with whole-rupee prices so that the rate problem remains separate from formal decimal calculations.

Time, shapes and pictographs

Connect a.m. and p.m. with the day, and read the 24-hour format used on timetables. 14:30 is 2:30 p.m. From 14:30 to 15:10 is forty minutes: thirty minutes to 15:00 and ten more to 15:10.

Explore right, acute and obtuse angles through corners and turns. A radius joins a circle's centre to its boundary; a diameter goes through the centre from one side to the other. Draw circles with a compass when that is part of the lesson. Fold shapes, investigate nets and read simple maps. Naming an angle does not mean every child must already measure it in degrees.

Read pictographs by using the key. If each flower picture represents four plants, three pictures represent twelve plants. Ask which category has most, how two categories compare and whether the key changes the count.

Indie the honeybee checks her work with an estimate and another operation. Encourage your child to explain one useful check, rather than doing every question twice without a purpose.

3 × 24 = 72. Three groups of twenty give sixty and three groups of four give twelve. 60 + 12 = 72. Check: 72 ÷ 3 = 24.
