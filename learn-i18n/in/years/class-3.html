Class 3 is part of the Preparatory Stage. Children are usually about 8–9 and do more written work, but objects, drawings and conversation still matter. A correct answer is useful; understanding how it was found is what helps with the next problem.

Current NCERT Maths Mela Class 3 work develops numbers to 1,000. The national NIPUN Bharat mission aims for children to read and write numbers up to about 9,999 and to add, subtract and begin to multiply confidently by the end of Class 3. Some school books therefore go further with four-digit numbers. Check your child's book before choosing practice.

Use place value to read and compare

In 608, the 6 represents six hundreds, the 0 records no tens and the 8 represents eight ones. Write 608 = 600 + 8. The zero holds the tens place; leaving it out would give a different number.

Compare 608 and 680 by looking first at the hundreds and then the tens. Both have six hundreds, but eight tens are more than no tens, so 680 is greater. Children also meet the comparison signs > and <. Read the whole statement aloud so the sign has meaning.

Practise counting forwards and backwards across a hundred, such as 298, 299, 300, 301. If the school introduces numbers to 9,999, extend the place-value chart to thousands using the same reasoning.

Add and subtract three-digit numbers

Keep hundreds, tens and ones aligned in written calculations. Regrouping changes how a number is represented, not its value. Ten ones can become a ten; ten tens can become a hundred.

Worked example: 347 + 285

The ones give 7 + 5 = 12: write two ones and regroup one ten. The tens give 4 + 8 + 1 = 13 tens: write three tens and regroup one hundred. The hundreds give 3 + 2 + 1 = 6. The total is 632. Check with a different method: 347 + 200 + 80 + 5 = 547 + 80 + 5 = 632.

Worked example: 500 − 278

Represent 500 as five hundreds. Regroup one hundred into ten tens, then one of those tens into ten ones. You now have four hundreds, nine tens and ten ones. Subtract two hundreds, seven tens and eight ones. Two hundreds, two tens and two ones remain: 222. Check: 278 + 222 = 500.

When zero appears in a subtraction, draw the regrouping first. Crossing out digits without knowing what they represent can hide a misunderstanding.

Build tables from known facts

Tables up to 10 support much of the year's multiplication and division. Some schools practise further tables; that is a school choice rather than a reason to rush past understanding. Ask mixed questions as well as reciting a table in order.

For 6 × 7, use five groups of seven and one more group: 35 + 7 = 42. For 8 × 4, double 4 × 4: 16 + 16 = 32. Draw arrays to check the groups. Talk about multiplying by 1, multiplying by 0 and the place-value patterns when multiplying whole numbers by 10 or 100.

Children can extend equal-group thinking to two- or three-digit numbers multiplied by one digit. For example, 23 × 4 is twenty multiplied by four plus three multiplied by four: 80 + 12 = 92. Use the written method your child's teacher expects, while keeping this meaning visible.

Connect division with multiplication

Division can mean sharing into a given number of equal groups or finding how many groups of a given size fit. For 42 ÷ 6, ask how many groups of six make forty-two. Since 6 × 7 = 42, the answer is seven.

Not every collection divides equally. Share seventeen counters between four toys. Each gets four and one remains. Record 17 ÷ 4 = 4 remainder 1. Check with 4 × 4 + 1 = 17. Discuss what the remainder means in the story: a leftover counter is different from a roti that can be cut and shared.

Explore familiar fractions

Fold a paper circle into two equal parts. Each part is a half. Fold another into four equal parts. Each is a quarter, and three of those parts make three quarters. The parts must be equal: any two pieces do not automatically make halves.

Children also explore thirds and fractions of collections. Half of twelve buttons is six because twelve shared into two equal groups gives six in each. A quarter of twelve is three. Keep the whole clear: half of a small roti and half of a large roti describe the same fraction, but not the same amount of food.

At this stage, focus on equal parts and what familiar fractions represent. Formal terms such as numerator and denominator are developed in Class 5 NCERT work, so they need not be the starting point here.

Measure and use time and money

Use centimetres and metres for length, grams and kilograms for mass, and millilitres and litres for capacity. A ruler, a packet label and a measuring jug connect the units to real quantities. Choose suitable units: a pencil's length is better described in centimetres than kilometres.

Practise telling time to five-minute intervals when introduced in the school lesson. Five past, ten past and quarter past describe positions of the minute hand. From 3:15 to 3:45 is thirty minutes. Move the hands or mark the interval on a timeline to check.

Read small bills, add costs and calculate change. Three notebooks at ₹20 each cost ₹60. Paying ₹100 leaves ₹40. Ask your child to check both the multiplication and the change, and to include ₹ in the final money answer.

Shapes, maps and information

Describe flat shapes and solid objects. A box has faces, edges and corners; a drawing of a rectangle is flat. Explore symmetry by folding a picture and looking for matching halves. Follow a simple map of the classroom using landmarks and directions.

Make a tally or a simple picture record of favourite fruits. If a picture stands for more than one child, read its key carefully. Ask what the information shows and what it does not tell you. Formal bar graphs are not a core requirement to add to this NCERT Class 3 guide.

Indie the honeybee checks a division by multiplying. Encourage your child to choose a check that fits the calculation rather than repeating the same steps without looking for errors.

6 × 7 = 42. Five rows contain 35 flowers and one more row contains seven: 35 + 7 = 42. Count the array or use 42 ÷ 6 = 7 to check.
