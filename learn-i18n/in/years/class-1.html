Class 1 maths begins with things children can see and handle: buttons, pencils, leaves, toys and their own fingers. The aim is not to fill a copy with answers as quickly as possible. It is to understand what a number means and what changes when we add or take something away.

Most children in Class 1 are about 6–7. Schools use different books and sequences, so follow your child's current lesson rather than treating this page as a weekly syllabus. NCERT's Joyful Mathematics gives children opportunities to explore ideas through pictures, activities and familiar situations.

Count objects, not only number names

A child may recite numbers confidently and still lose track while counting objects. Put out six spoons. Ask your child to touch or move each spoon as they count. Then ask, “How many altogether?” The last number said tells us the total.

Begin with small collections, including numbers from 1 to 9, and introduce zero through an empty group. Build towards counting to 20 and recognising larger numbers as the school introduces them. Children meet number sequences towards 50, 99 and 100, but understanding a small collection matters more than racing through a long sequence.

  • Ask for the number before, after or between two numbers.
  • Match a written number to the correct collection.
  • Compare two groups: which has more, fewer or the same number?
  • Practise number names in words alongside numerals.

For example, put seven buttons in one group and five in another. Match them in pairs. Two buttons remain in the first group, so seven is more than five. Your child does not need a comparison symbol to explain this.

Make a ten

Tens and ones are easier to understand when a ten is something children can hold. Bundle ten sticks together with an elastic band. Put three loose sticks beside the bundle. You have one ten and three ones: 13.

Now compare 13 and 16. Both have one bundle of ten; the loose sticks tell us which is larger. Keep the bundles visible while your child explains. Later, they can draw a bundle and dots instead of handling the sticks.

Counting in 2s, 5s or 10s can grow out of real groups: pairs of socks, fingers on hands or bundles of sticks. Use small groups first. Words such as first, second and third describe position rather than how many there are. Line up toys and ask which is first or fifth; extend towards tenth when your child is ready.

Change the arrangement without changing the objects. Ask whether six buttons spread out are still six. Let your child count to check rather than telling them immediately.

Add and take away

Start with calculations using small numbers, then extend within 20 as understanding grows. Explain the signs as part of a story: + means adding, − means taking away or finding a difference, and = means the two sides have the same value.

Worked example: 8 + 5

Put out eight counters and another five. Move two from the group of five to complete a ten with the eight. Three remain. So 8 + 5 = 10 + 3 = 13. Your child can also count on five steps from eight: 9, 10, 11, 12, 13.

Worked example: 14 − 6

Make fourteen with a ten and four ones. Remove four ones to reach ten, then remove two more. Eight remain, so 14 − 6 = 8. Check by putting six back: 8 + 6 = 14.

Number bonds help children see different ways to make the same total. Ten can be 6 + 4, 7 + 3 or 5 + 5. Give your child ten objects and ask them to split the collection in different ways. Written two-digit column calculations are not the starting point for these ideas.

Notice shapes and compare measurements

A ball and a box behave differently. Roll them, slide them and talk about what happens. Look for circles, triangles, squares and rectangles in pictures and around the classroom. Ask your child to describe what they notice rather than relying only on a memorised name.

Compare longer and shorter pencils, heavier and lighter objects, or containers that hold more and less. Line up objects at one end before comparing length. Measure a table with handspans or a path with footsteps. If two people get different answers, compare the size of their hands or steps.

Daily routines also give meaning to time: before school, after lunch, yesterday and tomorrow. Discuss days of the week and the order of events. Formal clock reading may be introduced differently by different schools; it need not replace this practical sense of time.

Money, patterns and simple records

Let your child look at familiar coins and notes with you. The value matters, not the physical size. In a pretend shop, an item costing ₹5 can be paid for with five ₹1 coins. Keep the task small enough that your child can count the whole amount.

Make repeating patterns with leaves and stones, or red and blue blocks. Ask what repeats and what comes next. A pattern such as leaf, stone, leaf, stone continues with a leaf after the next stone. Children can also record choices with one mark for each person: one mark for each child who chooses mango, for example.

Help without taking over

Choose one short activity and stop while your child still feels comfortable. Read a word problem aloud if the reading is getting in the way. Let them explain in the language they know best, then help connect the idea to the words used at school. Praise a careful check or a clear explanation, not only a correct answer.

Indie the honeybee counts flowers one at a time. She can make a mistake and count again; so can your child. If schoolwork repeatedly causes distress, share a specific example with the teacher rather than asking for more practice of the same kind.

7 + 6 = 13. Move three from the six to make ten with the seven. Ten and the remaining three make thirteen. Count all thirteen flowers to check.
