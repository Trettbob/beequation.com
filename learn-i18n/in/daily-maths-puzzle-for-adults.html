A cup of chai, a quiet moment before the school run or a break in the staffroom can be enough time for a small maths puzzle. You do not need to remember every school method. Start with what you notice, try a calculation and check whether it fits.

This is a break for you, not another test to give your child. There is no need to turn your score into a target for the family. You can enjoy a puzzle privately, discuss a solution with a colleague or invite someone to find a different method.

How the honeycomb works

Look at the five numbers. Choose three different cells: one number must be the sum or product of the other two. You do not have to use all five numbers, and a number in a cell cannot be used twice. The two unused cells are distractors.

In an addition puzzle, look for a relationship such as 37 + 18 = 55. In a multiplication puzzle, look for one such as 12 × 9 = 108. The aim is not simply to select the largest number: you must show the equation that connects your three choices.

Before doing a long calculation, ask whether the answer is plausible. Two positive numbers add to more than either one; multiplication facts can also give you useful size clues.

A five-minute routine

  1. Notice. Read all five numbers. Look for doubles, round numbers or a familiar table fact.
  2. Choose. Pick a likely answer and two numbers that might produce it.
  3. Calculate. Use a method you can explain, with rough work if useful.
  4. Check. Reverse the calculation or use a second method.
  5. Stop or explore. Enjoy the solved puzzle, or look for another valid relationship.

If nothing stands out, work systematically. Test one pair at a time rather than repeating the same guesses. For addition, subtract one possible addend from a candidate total and see whether the difference is another cell.

Three puzzles, three useful methods

Bridge through a ten

The numbers are 37, 18, 55, 26 and 70. Try 37 + 18. Split 18 into 10 and 8: 37 + 10 = 47, then 47 + 8 = 55. The three cells are 37, 18 and 55.

A second route is 37 + 3 = 40, then add the remaining 15 to make 55. Check backwards: 55 − 18 = 37. Both routes establish the same relationship.

Use a nearby multiplication fact

The numbers are 12, 9, 108, 14 and 15. Ten groups of 12 make 120, so nine groups make one group of 12 less: 120 − 12 = 108. Therefore 12 × 9 = 108.

Check with division: 108 ÷ 12 = 9. You can also split 12 into 10 and 2: 9 × 10 = 90 and 9 × 2 = 18, giving 90 + 18 = 108.

Make a friendly number

The numbers are 28, 19, 46, 35 and 63. Move 2 from the 35 to the 28: 28 + 35 becomes 30 + 33, which is 63. The total stays the same because one addend increases by 2 while the other decreases by 2.

Check by partitioning instead: 20 + 30 = 50 and 8 + 5 = 13, so the total is 63. Or subtract 35 from 63 to recover 28.

Indie is a honeybee, and she enjoys finding a second route. She does not mind rough work: a few written numbers can make your reasoning easier to check.

Choose the kind of break you want

Try Daily for a regular puzzle, or explore Blitz, Hard or Pro when you want a different challenge. These are choices, not a compulsory ladder. A more demanding mode is not automatically a better break. Read the game's instructions and choose what suits the time and attention you have.

At home, you might talk through a solution after finishing. Keep it conversational: “I used a nearby table fact” invites discussion more readily than “How quickly can you do this?” A child can listen without being expected to solve an adult puzzle.

Make a paper puzzle

Choose an equation first: 24 + 16 = 40. Put those three numbers in separate cells, then add two distractors, such as 7 and 11. Shuffle the positions. Before sharing it, check the possible pairs: in this set, 24 and 16 give 40; none of the other pairs has its sum in another cell.

If you make a puzzle with several valid answers, say so. That can become an enjoyable search, but it is unfair to reject a correct equation just because it is not the one you intended. The printable puzzle pack gives you a ready-made alternative.
